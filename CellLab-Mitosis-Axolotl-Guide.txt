2. What it feels like to listen

• Enter the lesson quietly. Tap “Hear overview” to start the axolotl. Play the hook the user already likes, then the new overview as one continuous listening experience. Hold the layout steady. While he speaks, animate a very small mouth movement or gill motion using the existing art and crossfade the speaker icon to a stop square. With Reduce Motion enabled use a still face and the icon change.
• When the overview ends, leave a short breath (about 350 ms), then narrate the visible phase. End the phase clip and wait on that card. The learner decides when to turn the page. Keep the phase name and existing “1 / 5” indicator visible while they listen.
• While narration is on, a swipe or phase-dot tap selects the new card. Stop the outgoing clip as soon as selection changes. Start the newly selected clip after the page settles (roughly 200–300 ms). Use the settled selection as the single trigger. A fast swipe across three cards should produce one clean start on the last card. A swipe during the overview skips straight to the chosen phase. Expanding “Pack it up” or scrolling within an explanation keeps the current audio position.
• When a phase clip ends, the guide offers “Replay Prophase” (using the current phase name). Keep a separate compact Stop control available between clips while swipe narration is enabled; while speaking, the main control itself can say “Stop.” Stop cancels both audio and any pending auto-start, and makes future swipes quiet. After stopping on a phase, offer “Hear [phase]” to start there. Make “Replay overview” available as a secondary action in the guide’s small menu. After the last phase, wait for the learner to replay, browse, or choose Play game.
• Lower sections and reference sheets are opt-in. Tapping their speaker replaces the current audio and exits swipe narration. Give that section a visible Stop while it speaks. Opening a sheet, starting the game, leaving the page, or backgrounding the app cancels outgoing audio and pending callbacks. Coming back is quiet. The game has its own opt-in from the instruction area.
• In the game, speak the current instruction when guidance is turned on; after that, speak the cue for each newly available action or newly visible completion pop-up once. Learners can act immediately. Advancing or dismissing a pop-up interrupts obsolete speech. Restart clears played-event markers. Manual replay is always allowed.
• Share one playback owner across the active Mitosis experience. Audio completion, clip changes, and mouth animation follow actual player state. Cancel stale delayed work and ignore completion callbacks from interrupted clips. Respect audio interruptions. Give controls descriptive VoiceOver names (“Hear mitosis overview,” “Stop narration,” “Replay metaphase”) and at least 44 × 44 pt hit targets. Expose each clip’s text as an on-demand transcript using the same script entry. Keep the existing short on-screen summaries.

RECORD lesson.overview | Opening · Listen
What do axolotl tails and the skin on your nose have in common? Dividing cells!

They help regrow a lost tail or replace the skin you shed every day. The part where the cell separates its copied DNA? That’s mitosis!

Let’s zoom in on a human skin cell. We start with one nucleus and forty-six chromosomes. The cell already copied each chromosome during interphase, so each one has two copies joined together. By the end of mitosis, those copies have separated. Now there are two nuclei, each with a full set of forty-six. How does the cell sort them? Swipe through the five steps with me!

RECORD reference.human-count | Optional · Human chromosome count illustration
Most of your body cells have forty-six chromosomes. That’s twenty-three pairs. For each pair, you inherited one chromosome from the egg and one from the sperm. Before mitosis, the cell copies each of the forty-six during a stage called S phase. Now there are ninety-two sister chromatids. Mitosis sorts the copies so each new nucleus gets forty-six chromosomes again.

RECORD reference.chromosome-eleven | Optional · What’s actually on chromosome eleven?
What do breathing and lunch have in common? Try chromosome eleven. It carries lots of genes, stretches of DNA with particular instructions. One tells cells in your pancreas how to make insulin, which helps manage blood sugar after a meal. Another, called H-B-B, gives instructions for part of hemoglobin, the protein your red blood cells use to carry oxygen. Even the skin cell patching your knee passes on both genes when it divides.

RECORD reference.count-history | Optional · Who counted all forty-six?
How many chromosomes do humans usually have? For about thirty years, the accepted answer was forty-eight. Then researchers got a clearer look at human chromosomes during metaphase. They could spread them out and count them. In nineteen fifty-six, they published the answer we use today. Forty-six. Imagine opening your biology book and having to fix the answer key.

RECORD reference.players | Meet the key players
Tap a name to meet the division crew. Who moves the chromosomes, and what holds the copies together? Pick a player and I’ll show you its part.

RECORD reference.cytokinesis | Actin and myosin
How does one animal cell actually become two? Just under the cell membrane, actin and myosin form a ring. As the ring tightens, it pinches the cell in two. That’s cytokinesis!

RECORD game.prophase.start | Prophase · Game begins
Your turn. We’ve given you four chromosomes to keep track of. Tap each loose bundle of DNA to pack it up. Watch for the two copies in every X.

RECORD game.prophase.packed | Prophase · All four bundles packed
Tap the two centrosomes. They move toward opposite ends of the cell. Those ends are called poles, and each will get its own set of chromosomes.

RECORD game.prophase.result | Prophase · Completion pop-up
How much DNA would you be packing in a human cell? Even before it makes a copy, a typical body cell has about six feet of DNA tucked inside its nucleus. Imagine fitting a jump rope into a speck you need a microscope to see.

RECORD game.prometaphase.start | Prometaphase · Stage begins
Tap Open envelope. That’s the membrane around the nucleus. Once it breaks down, spindle fibers can reach the chromosomes.

RECORD game.prometaphase.envelope-open | Prometaphase · Envelope open
Choose a pole, then tap all four chromosomes. Switch poles and tap them again. See those gold dots? They’re the kinetochores, the places where spindle fibers attach.

RECORD game.prometaphase.result | Prometaphase · Completion pop-up
Pick any X and trace its two connections. One sister is hooked to the left side. Her sister’s hooked to the right. Same starting point, different rides home.

RECORD game.metaphase.start | Metaphase · Stage begins
Bring the four X’s to the dashed line. You can drag them or give each one a tap. We drew you a guide. The cell does this freehand.

RECORD game.metaphase.aligned | Metaphase · All four aligned
They look lined up. But is every sister connected? Tap Run checkpoint.

RECORD game.metaphase.result | Metaphase · Completion pop-up
All connected. In a real cell, even one sister with an empty attachment site can delay the split for everybody. The cell takes attendance very seriously.

RECORD game.anaphase.start | Anaphase · Stage begins
Pick one X to watch and tap Separate sisters. See if you can follow both copies.

RECORD game.anaphase.result | Anaphase · Completion pop-up
Count them now. Four on the left, four on the right. How did four chromosomes become eight? A second ago each X counted as one. Now its two copies are apart, so each counts as a chromosome. The amount of DNA stayed the same.

RECORD game.telophase.start | Telophase · Stage begins
Tap each group to give its chromosomes a new nucleus. Watch those tightly packed shapes loosen into threads.

RECORD game.telophase.result | Telophase · Completion pop-up
Four chromosomes in this nucleus, four in that one. Each group has one copy from every X you started with.

RECORD game.complete | Mitosis complete · Final screen
You’ve made two nuclei. Notice there’s still one cell around them. To finish dividing, an animal cell pinches through the middle and splits its contents between two new cells. That’s cytokinesis. It can start while mitosis is wrapping up. Remember the skin on your nose? Making new cells like this helps replace the ones you shed.

RECORD game.growth | Optional · The growth-spurt connection
Ever outgrow a pair of pants that still fits at the waist? As a kid, you had strips of cartilage called growth plates near the ends of your leg bones. Cells there divided, and some grew larger. New bone gradually replaced that cartilage, helping your legs grow longer. Those mysteriously shorter pants? Your cells had something to do with that.
